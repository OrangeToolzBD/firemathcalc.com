Rule of 72 Calculator

This rule of 72 calculator estimates how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 8%, that gives 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. The exact answer, ln 2 ÷ ln 1.08, is 9.01 years, so the shortcut is very close at that rate. The calculator shows both figures for any rate you enter.

What is the rule of 72?

The rule of 72 is a mental-math shortcut for compound growth: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ yearly rate (in percent). It also works backwards. To double in a set number of years, you need a rate of about 72 ÷ years. It assumes the rate stays steady and that growth compounds once a year.

Some quick examples:

  • At 6%, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.
  • At 9%, about 8 years.
  • To double in 10 years, you need about 7.2% a year.

The number 72 is used because it divides evenly by many common rates (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12) and gives close answers for the rates people use most.

The exact formula is years = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + rate), which the calculator shows next to the estimate. For longer-range projections with deposits, use the compound interest calculator. To find the rate behind a past result, use the CAGR calculator.

How accurate is the rule of 72?

The rule of 72 is most accurate for rates around 8%, and it gets worse as rates move far from that. It slightly overstates the time at low rates and understates it at high rates. For everyday rates between about 4% and 12%, the error is well under half a year.

The bigger uncertainty is usually the rate itself, not the rule. Investment returns vary from year to year, so any doubling time for an investment is an estimate based on your assumption.

Rule of 72 for inflation and debt

The rule of 72 also works in reverse for inflation, and in the same direction for debt. At a steady 3% inflation, prices double, and a dollar’s buying power halves, in about 24 years (exactly 23.45). A debt growing at 18% with no payments would double in about 4 years.

Inflation example with real data: by the BLS CPI-U, prices rose an average of about 2.61% a year from 1990 to 2025. The rule of 72 suggests doubling in about 27.6 years. The actual index went from 130.7 to 321.943 over those 35 years, about 2.46 times. That is consistent, since prices passed double and kept rising.

The inflation calculator gives exact figures for any pair of years.

For debt, the rule shows why high-interest balances grow quickly if left unpaid. The exact time depends on how often interest compounds and on any payments or fees, so treat the estimate as a rough warning, not a payoff plan.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the yearly interest rate, entered as a whole number. At 6%, 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years to double. To find the rate needed to double in a set time, divide 72 by the number of years instead: doubling in 9 years needs about 8%.

How many years will it take $100,000 to double at 12% per year?

By the rule of 72, 72 ÷ 12 = 6.00 years, when $100,000 would reach about $200,000. The exact answer with yearly compounding is ln 2 ÷ ln 1.12, or 6.12 years. The shortcut is slightly quick at this rate.

What will $50,000 be worth in 20 years?

It depends on the rate. At an assumed 7% a year, compounded yearly, $50,000 grows to about $193,484, close to two doublings (the rule of 72 gives about 10.3 years per doubling). At 5% it grows to about $132,665. Inflation reduces what those amounts will buy.

Does the rule of 72 actually work?

Yes, as an estimate. Between about 4% and 12%, it is within half a year of the exact doubling time. It is least accurate at very low or very high rates. It also assumes a steady rate, which investments rarely deliver, so use it for quick comparisons, not precise planning.
