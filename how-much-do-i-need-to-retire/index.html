How Much Do I Need to Retire?

To answer how much do I need to retire, divide the yearly spending your savings must cover by the withdrawal rate you plan to use. At 4%, that means 25 times your spending: $40,000 a year needs $1,000,000. Social Security and pensions reduce what your savings must cover, and a lower withdrawal rate raises the target.

How much money do I need to retire?

Start with what you will spend each year in retirement, not what you earn now. Subtract the income you expect from Social Security, a pension or part-time work. Then divide the gap by a withdrawal rate. Savings needed = (yearly spending − other income) ÷ withdrawal rate. The withdrawal rate is your assumption, and it changes the answer a lot.

Step 1: estimate spending

List what you spend now and adjust it for retirement. Some costs may end, such as commuting, a mortgage that will be paid off, or retirement saving itself. Others may rise, such as healthcare, travel or help at home. Your current budget is the most reliable starting point. Income replacement rules of thumb are a rougher shortcut.

Step 2: subtract your other income

Social Security, pensions and annuity payments reduce the amount your portfolio must supply. The next sections show how much that can change the number.

Step 3: choose a withdrawal rate and divide

A higher rate gives a smaller target but a higher chance of running short. A lower rate gives a larger, more cautious target. Retiring early usually argues for a lower rate, because the money has to last longer.

Two tools can help. The FIRE calculator uses this multiple directly and adds the years needed to get there. The retirement calculator takes a different approach. It works out the lump sum that pays your yearly gap for a set number of years at a return you choose, so the money is planned to run down to zero by the end.

Both give estimates in today’s dollars when you use returns after inflation. Neither includes taxes, which apply to withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts.

Where does the 4% rule come from?

The 4% rule comes from research on historical U.S. market returns. The two best-known papers are William Bengen’s 1994 article in the Journal of Financial Planning and a 1998 AAII Journal article by Philip Cooley, Carl Hubbard and Daniel Walz, often called the Trinity study. Both tested past withdrawal rates. Neither guarantees future results.

Bengen, 1994

In “Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data” (October 1994; reprinted by the Financial Planning Association in 2004), Bengen worked with a portfolio of 50% common stocks and 50% intermediate-term Treasuries. He used Ibbotson Associates data and tested retirements beginning each year from 1926.

His method was to withdraw a percentage of the starting balance in year one, then raise that dollar amount with inflation each year. At 4%, no historical case ran out of money in under 33 years. At 4.25%, a portfolio could be exhausted in as little as 28 years. He described a 5% start as risky and 6% or more as gambling. He assumed the money sat in tax-deferred accounts.

The Trinity study, 1998

“Retirement Savings: Choosing a Withdrawal Rate That Is Sustainable” (AAII Journal, February 1998) tested withdrawal rates from 3% to 12% over payout periods of 15 to 30 years. It covered several stock and bond mixes, using S&P 500 and long-term, high-grade corporate bond returns from 1926 to 1995. The study made no adjustment for taxes or transaction costs.

Its inflation-adjusted table reports the share of past 30-year periods (41 in total) in which a 4% withdrawal left money at the end:

Portfolio mix4% withdrawal, raised with inflation, 30 years
100% stocks95%
75% stocks / 25% bonds98%
50% stocks / 50% bonds95%
25% stocks / 75% bonds71%
100% bonds20%

The authors concluded that early retirees expecting long payout periods should plan on lower withdrawal rates. Both studies looked at periods of about 30 years, which is shorter than many early retirements. Future returns may differ from the past.

How do Social Security and pensions lower the number?

Every dollar of reliable yearly income you receive is a dollar your savings do not have to supply. At a 4% withdrawal rate, each $1,000 a year of Social Security or pension income lowers the savings you need by about $25,000. That makes your benefit estimate one of the most important inputs in the whole calculation.

A worked example: you plan to spend $60,000 a year.

  • With no other income, you would need $1,500,000 at 4%.
  • If Social Security and a pension together pay $20,000 a year, the gap falls to $40,000, and the target falls to $1,000,000.

The retirement calculator uses the same $60,000 and $20,000 as defaults. Over a 25-year retirement at an assumed 4% return, it finds that $624,883 would fund the $40,000 yearly gap if the money is planned to run out at the end. Without the other income, the figure rises to $937,325.

Use your own benefit estimate, not an average. Your Social Security statement at ssa.gov shows your estimate.

If your benefit starts years after you stop work, your savings must cover the full gap until it begins. You can model that as a shorter, larger bridge withdrawal followed by a smaller long-term one.

What about healthcare before 65?

If you retire before 65, you need your own health coverage until Medicare begins. Medicare generally covers people 65 or older who meet citizenship or residency requirements, with earlier eligibility for some people with disabilities or certain conditions. Premiums and out-of-pocket costs for the years in between belong in your spending estimate.

The common routes are:

  • COBRA: continuing your former employer’s plan for a limited time.
  • The Health Insurance Marketplace: HealthCare.gov says that losing job-based coverage when you retire lets you enroll from 60 days before to 60 days after you leave work. You may qualify for premium tax credits based on household income and size.
  • A spouse’s employer plan, if one is available.

HealthCare.gov also notes that you cannot drop COBRA outside Open Enrollment to switch to a Marketplace plan, unless the COBRA coverage is ending. During Open Enrollment you can make that switch.

Because Marketplace savings depend on income, the way you draw money in early retirement can affect your premiums. That is a reason to plan withdrawals and taxes together, possibly with a professional.

A practical approach is to add a separate line for pre-65 health costs. Treat it as a bridge that ends at 65, rather than raising your spending for your whole retirement.

Frequently asked questions

Can you retire on $1.5 million comfortably?

That depends on your spending and other income. At a 4% first-year withdrawal, $1.5 million supports about $60,000 a year. At 3.5%, it supports about $52,500. Add Social Security or a pension on top. If your planned spending fits within those figures and your retirement is not unusually long, the math may work. Taxes and healthcare costs still need to be counted.

Can I retire at 60 with $500,000 in savings?

A 4% first-year withdrawal from $500,000 is $20,000 a year. Whether that is enough depends on your other income and costs. If Social Security or a pension covers most of your spending, it may work. From 60 you also need health coverage until Medicare, which generally starts at 65. Run your own figures in the retirement calculator.

What withdrawal rate should I use for an early retirement?

No single rate fits everyone. Bengen’s research tested at least 30 years, and the Trinity study tested up to 30 years, while an early retirement can last much longer. The Trinity authors concluded that early retirees should plan on lower rates. Many people test 3% to 3.5% alongside 4% to see how the target changes. The rate is always your own assumption.
