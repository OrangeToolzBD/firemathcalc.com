FIRE Calculator

This FIRE calculator turns your yearly retirement spending into a target: spending divided by your withdrawal rate. At $40,000 a year and 4%, that is $1,000,000. It then counts the years your savings need to get there at the return you assume, and shows each step, so you can change any number and see why the answer moves.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Fire Math Calc

What is a FIRE number?

Your FIRE number is the portfolio size that could pay your yearly spending at the withdrawal rate you pick. The formula is FIRE number = yearly spending ÷ withdrawal rate. At a 4% rate that works out to 25 times your spending. At 3.5% it is about 28.6 times.

FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. The number is not a promise that the money will last. It is a planning target, and it depends heavily on the withdrawal rate, which is your assumption. The rate you choose changes the target more than most people expect:

Withdrawal rate (your assumption)FIRE number for $40,000 a year
3%$1,333,333
3.5%$1,142,857
4%$1,000,000
5%$800,000

The 4% default comes from research on past markets. In the October 1994 Journal of Financial Planning, William Bengen tested a first-year withdrawal of 4% of the portfolio, raised with inflation each year after, on a portfolio split evenly between stocks and intermediate-term Treasuries. For retirements starting from 1926 onward, he found no case where that plan ran out of money in under 33 years. A first-year rate of 4.25% could run out in as little as 28 years.

That was a 30-year question for people retiring around 60 to 65. Retiring at 40 may mean 50 years or more of withdrawals, which is one reason many early retirees consider a lower rate. The how much do I need to retire guide covers the research in more detail.

How long will it take me to reach FIRE?

The time to FIRE depends on three things: what you have invested now, what you add each year, and the return after inflation you assume. The calculator grows your balance one year at a time, adds your yearly investment at the end of each year, and stops when the balance crosses your FIRE number.

Inside the year where the balance crosses the target, it estimates the fraction of the year by straight-line interpolation. That is why answers come out as 16.9 years rather than a whole number.

The return you enter is after inflation, so every figure is in today’s dollars. A $1,000,000 target means $1,000,000 of today’s buying power, not a nominal figure that inflation will shrink. If you prefer to think in nominal returns, subtract your inflation assumption first.

Your savings amount usually matters more than the return. With $100,000 invested and a $1,000,000 target, here is how the time changes with the return alone, at $30,000 a year of new investing:

Return after inflation (your assumption)Years to $1,000,000

If the balance stops growing, for example with a 0% return and nothing added, the tool says the target is not reached rather than printing a misleading number.

Worked example: $40,000 a year of spending

With the calculator’s default inputs, someone spending $40,000 a year who uses a 4% withdrawal rate has a FIRE number of $1,000,000. Starting from $100,000 invested, adding $30,000 a year, at an assumed 5% return after inflation, they reach it in about 16.9 years. Starting at 32, that is age 48.

Here is the math step by step:

  1. FIRE number: $40,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,000,000.
  2. Year 1: $100,000 × 1.05 + $30,000 = $135,000.
  3. Year 2: $135,000 × 1.05 + $30,000 = $171,750. The same step repeats each year.
  4. Crossing year: the balance passes $1,000,000 during year 17, and the interpolation places the crossing at 16.94 years.
  5. Age at FI: 32 + 16.94, rounded down, is 48.

A second check from the tool’s tests: someone starting from zero who spends $50,000 a year needs $1,250,000 at 4%. Investing $20,000 a year at an assumed 7% real return, they get there in about 24.9 years.

Both examples leave out taxes, fees and any change in spending. They show how the formula works, not what any market will return.

Lean, regular and fat FIRE compared

Lean FIRE, regular FIRE and fat FIRE are informal community labels for low, middle and high retirement budgets. They have no official definitions or dollar thresholds. The math is identical for all three: only the spending figure changes, and the FIRE number scales with it.

The table below uses sample budgets chosen to illustrate the range. Each assumes $100,000 invested today, $30,000 invested per year and a 5% real return. Every input is your assumption to change.

Lean FIRE leaves little room for surprises, such as a large medical bill or a bad run in the markets early on. Fat FIRE takes longer to reach but leaves more room to cut back if needed.

Barista FIRE sits in between: you leave full-time work but keep part-time income that covers part of your spending. To model it here, enter only the spending your portfolio must cover. If part-time pay covers $15,000 of a $40,000 budget, enter $25,000. The Coast FIRE calculator covers the related idea of stopping new contributions altogether.

What does this calculator leave out?

This FIRE calculator shows the core arithmetic only. It uses one steady return every year and leaves out taxes, fees, the order of returns, account withdrawal rules, healthcare costs and spending that changes over time. Each of these can move your real number, so treat the result as a starting estimate.

Sequence-of-returns risk

The calculator assumes the same return every year. Real markets are uneven, and the order of returns matters once you start withdrawing. A fall early in retirement forces you to sell more shares to fund the same spending, and those shares are no longer there for the recovery.

Bengen’s 1994 study shows this clearly. The 1973–74 downturn, which came with high inflation, shortened portfolio lives even for people who had retired many years before it. At a 5% first-year rate, some retirements that began in the late 1960s and early 1970s had only about 20 years of funds. The same average return can give very different results depending on when the bad years arrive.

Taxes and account rules

Withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts are generally taxed. The IRS also adds a 10% additional tax on most distributions before age 59½ (page reviewed December 11, 2025). Early retirees often look at the exceptions, such as a series of substantially equal periodic payments, which applies to IRAs and to qualified plans.

Everything else

  • Investment fees, which lower your return every year.
  • Health insurance before Medicare, which generally starts at 65.
  • Social Security or a pension later in life, which reduces what the portfolio must pay.
  • Spending that rises or falls as you age.

The retirement calculator lets you subtract other income. The methodology page lists every formula. This is general education, not investment advice.

Frequently asked questions

How is a FIRE number calculated?

Divide the yearly spending you expect in retirement by the withdrawal rate you plan to use. At 4%, $40,000 of spending gives $1,000,000, and $60,000 gives $1,500,000. The withdrawal rate is an assumption, not a guarantee. A lower rate gives a larger, more cautious target, which is why many people planning a very long retirement test 3% or 3.5% as well.

Is the 4% rule safe for early retirement?

The research behind it looked at shorter retirements than most early retirees face. Bengen’s 1994 study used at least 30 years, and the 1998 Trinity study by Cooley, Hubbard and Walz tested payout periods of 15 to 30 years. Someone retiring at 40 may need their money for 50 years, so many early retirees consider a lower rate or plan to cut spending in bad years.

Does the FIRE calculator account for inflation?

Yes, as long as you enter a return after inflation, which is what the tool asks for. Using a real return keeps every figure in today’s dollars. Your FIRE number then stays at today’s buying power, and you do not need to raise your spending figure for future prices. If you have a nominal return in mind, subtract your inflation assumption before entering it.

What is the difference between FIRE and Coast FIRE?

FIRE means you have enough invested to stop working and live on withdrawals now. Coast FIRE means you have enough invested that, with no more contributions, growth alone should carry the balance to your FIRE number by a later retirement age. You still work to pay current bills, but you no longer need to save for retirement.
