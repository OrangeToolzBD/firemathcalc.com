ROI Calculator

This ROI investment calculator shows your total return and your annualized return. Invest $5,000 and get back $7,500, including income received, and your ROI is 50%, a $2,500 gain. Spread over 3 years, that is about 14.47% a year, which lets you compare investments held for different lengths of time.

How do you calculate ROI?

ROI, or return on investment, is your gain divided by what you put in: ROI = (amount returned − amount invested) ÷ amount invested × 100. “Amount returned” means the current value or sale price plus any income you received along the way, such as dividends or interest. With $5,000 in and $7,500 back, ROI = $2,500 ÷ $5,000 = 50%.

Getting the inputs right matters more than the formula:

  • Include all costs in the amount invested, such as purchase fees or commissions, if you want a true net figure.
  • Include all income in the amount returned, such as dividends, interest or rent, net of expenses.
  • Taxes are not included unless you subtract them yourself.

ROI can be negative. If $5,000 becomes $4,000, the ROI is −20%.

ROI is simple, and that is its weakness. A 50% gain over 3 years and a 50% gain over 15 years show the same ROI, but they are very different results. That is why the calculator also shows an annualized figure.

If you added money at different times, a single ROI figure can mislead. For regular deposits, the investment calculator separates your contributions from the growth.

What is annualized ROI?

Annualized ROI converts a total return into an equivalent yearly compound rate: annualized ROI = (amount returned ÷ amount invested)^(1 ÷ years) − 1. For $5,000 growing to $7,500 in 3 years: 1.5^(1/3) − 1 ≈ 14.47% a year. It is the same calculation as CAGR.

Annualizing makes comparisons fair. Compare two investments:

InvestmentTotal ROIYears heldAnnualized ROI
A50%314.47%
B60%68.15%

B has the higher total return, but A grew faster each year.

Annualized figures for short periods can be misleading. A 10% gain in two months annualizes to a very large number that is unlikely to repeat. For holdings of under a year, the total ROI is usually the more honest figure to report.

For more on the formula, and on why a compound rate differs from a simple average of yearly returns, see the CAGR calculator.

What counts as a good ROI?

There is no universal “good” ROI. A return is only meaningful next to the risk you took, the time involved and what the same money could have earned elsewhere. Useful comparisons include inflation over the same period and the return on a lower-risk option such as an insured savings account.

Two reference points, both dated:

  • Inflation: by the BLS CPI-U, prices rose about 2.53% a year on average from 2000 to 2025. A return below inflation lost buying power.
  • Savings: the FDIC national average savings rate was 0.38% as of August 17, 2026, and the 12-month CD average was 1.71%.

A higher expected return usually comes with more uncertainty, so a strong past ROI does not tell you what will happen next. This page does not rate or recommend any investment.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate ROI for an investment?

Subtract what you invested from what you got back, including any income, then divide by what you invested and multiply by 100. $5,000 returning $7,500 gives ($7,500 − $5,000) ÷ $5,000 = 50%. To compare with other holdings, annualize it: over 3 years that is about 14.47% a year.

What is a good ROI over 10 years?

It depends on the risk and the alternatives. One benchmark is inflation: from 2000 to 2025, CPI-U rose about 2.53% a year. At 7% a year, money roughly doubles in 10 years, a total ROI of about 97% with yearly compounding. Compare your result with a benchmark that suits the investment.

How much do I need to invest to make $1,000,000?

It depends on time and return. At an assumed 7% yearly return, compounded monthly, about $820 a month for 30 years or about $381 a month for 40 years reaches $1,000,000. Those figures ignore taxes, fees and inflation, and real returns vary.

How much will $10,000 invested be worth in 10 years?

At an assumed 7% yearly return, $10,000 grows to about $19,672 with yearly compounding or about $20,097 with monthly compounding. That is an ROI of roughly 97% to 101%. At lower returns the result is smaller, and no return is guaranteed.
