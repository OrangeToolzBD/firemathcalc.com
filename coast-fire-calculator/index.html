Coast FIRE Calculator

Coast FIRE is the point where your investments could grow to your full retirement number with no further contributions. Here is what Coast FIRE looks like in numbers: a 30-year-old who wants $1,000,000 by 60 needs about $231,377 invested today, assuming a 5% yearly return after inflation. This calculator finds your figure and your gap.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Fire Math Calc

What is Coast FIRE?

Coast FIRE means you have already saved enough for retirement, as long as the money is left to grow. From that point you only need to earn enough to cover your current spending. You stop saving for retirement and let compounding carry the balance to your full FIRE number by the age you choose.

The appeal is flexibility. Once you reach Coast FIRE, you can take a lower-paid job, work fewer hours or take a career break, with less worry that retirement falls behind. The price is that your balance has to stay invested, untouched, for many years, and the return along the way is not guaranteed.

Coast FIRE is a community term, not a financial standard. The calculator keeps it precise with one definition: the amount that, left to grow at your assumed return with no more contributions, reaches your FIRE number by your retirement age.

Your FIRE number here is the same as on the FIRE calculator: yearly retirement spending divided by your withdrawal rate. The default 4% rate traces back to William Bengen’s 1994 Journal of Financial Planning study. For past retirements from 1926 onward, a 4% first-year withdrawal raised with inflation lasted at least 33 years on the portfolio he tested. Both the rate and the return remain your assumptions.

How do you calculate your Coast FIRE number?

Divide your FIRE number by the growth factor for the years left until retirement: Coast FIRE number = FIRE number ÷ (1 + real return)^years to retirement. The FIRE number is yearly spending divided by your withdrawal rate. Because the return is after inflation, the result is in today’s dollars.

Worked example, using the calculator’s defaults:

  1. FIRE number: $40,000 of yearly spending ÷ 4% = $1,000,000.
  2. Years to grow: retiring at 60 from age 30 gives 30 years.
  3. Growth factor: 1.05 raised to the 30th power is about 4.32.
  4. Coast FIRE number: $1,000,000 ÷ 4.32 = $231,377.
  5. Gap: with $150,000 invested today, you still need about $81,377 more before you can coast.

You can check it the other way round. Left alone at 5% for 30 years, $150,000 grows to about $648,291, well short of $1,000,000. Starting from $231,377, it reaches the target.

More years to grow make the number fall fast. The same $1,000,000 target, retiring at 65 instead of 60 (35 years from age 30), needs about $181,290 today at 5%.

Once you reach your number, the tool shows how far ahead you are rather than how much is still to invest.

Coast FIRE vs Barista FIRE

Coast FIRE and Barista FIRE both mean working less before full retirement, but the money moves differently. At Coast FIRE you still earn all of your living costs, and your portfolio is left untouched to grow. At Barista FIRE, part-time income covers some spending and the portfolio pays the rest, so you are already withdrawing.

For Barista FIRE, enter only the part of your budget the portfolio must cover. If part-time pay covers $15,000 of $40,000, the portfolio needs to fund $25,000, which is $625,000 at a 4% withdrawal rate.

Withdrawing years before standard retirement age raises tax questions. The IRS generally adds a 10% additional tax on retirement account distributions before 59½, with listed exceptions (page reviewed December 11, 2025).

What happens if returns are lower?

A lower return raises your Coast FIRE number, sometimes sharply, because the shortfall compounds over decades. For the same $1,000,000 target and 30 years of growth, the amount needed today at a 3% real return is nearly double the figure at 5%. It is sensible to test a pessimistic return before deciding you have reached Coast FIRE.

The calculator also uses a single steady return. Real returns come in an uneven order, and a poor decade just before retirement can leave the balance short even when the long-run average looks fine. Bengen’s 1994 paper describes how the 1973–74 bear market, which came with high inflation, hurt retirement portfolios for years around it.

Two practical buffers: keep a margin above your Coast FIRE number rather than stopping exactly at it, and re-run the numbers every year with your actual balance. The compound interest calculator shows how much small additions still help. This is general education, not investment advice.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate your Coast FIRE number?

First find your FIRE number: yearly retirement spending divided by your withdrawal rate. Then divide it by (1 + real return) raised to the number of years until retirement. For $40,000 at 4%, retiring in 30 years at a 5% real return, that is $1,000,000 ÷ 4.32, or about $231,377. Enter your own figures above to get yours.

How long will $750,000 last in retirement at 62?

It depends mainly on how much you withdraw and what the money earns. Assuming a 3% return after inflation and withdrawals at the end of each year, $30,000 a year lasts about 47 years and $40,000 about 28 years. With no growth at all, $40,000 a year lasts 18.75 years. Social Security or a pension lowers the amount you need to withdraw.

How much do you need for Coast FIRE at 30?

It depends on your target and your assumed return. For a $1,000,000 FIRE number ($40,000 a year at 4%) and a 5% real return, a 30-year-old needs about $231,377 to coast to 60, or about $181,290 to coast to 65. At a 3% return those figures rise to $411,987 and $355,383. Test more than one return.

What makes a good Coast FIRE calculator?

Look for one that shows its formula, asks for a return after inflation so the answer is in today’s dollars, and labels the return and withdrawal rate as your assumptions. It should also show the gap between your savings and your number. This calculator does all of that and lists every formula on its methodology page, but no calculator can predict future returns.
