401(k) Calculator

This 401k calculator projects your balance from your pay, contribution rate, employer match and an assumed return. It caps your deferral at the 2026 IRS limit of $24,500 plus any catch-up. On an $80,000 salary, saving 10% with a 50% match up to 6% from $50,000 at 40, you reach about $785,184 by 65 at 6%.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Fire Math Calc

How much will my 401(k) be worth at retirement?

Your balance at retirement comes from your current balance, what you and your employer add each year, the years until you retire, and the return you assume. The calculator works one year at a time:

  1. Grow last year’s balance by your return.
  2. Add your contribution, capped at the IRS limit for your age that year.
  3. Add your employer’s match.

It can also raise your salary each year by an amount you choose.

Worked example, from the calculator’s tests:

  • Age 40, retiring at 65, so 25 years.
  • $50,000 already saved.
  • $80,000 salary, with no raises assumed.
  • You contribute 10%: $8,000 a year.
  • Your employer matches 50% of your contribution, up to 6% of pay: $2,400 a year.
  • An assumed 6% yearly return.

Result: about $785,184 at 65. Of that, $50,000 is the starting balance and $260,000 is new money: 25 years of $8,000 from you plus $2,400 from your employer. The rest is growth.

Contribution rate makes a large difference. In the same example, contributing 6% instead of 10% gives about $609,618. Contributing 4%, which also earns a smaller match, gives about $477,943.

Figures are before taxes and fees, and contributions are added at year end. Future years are held at 2026 limits. Returns are your assumption, not a forecast. To see what the balance might mean in today’s dollars, try the investment calculator with an inflation rate.

How does an employer match work?

An employer match is money your employer adds to your 401(k) based on what you contribute. A common way to describe it is “X% of your contribution, up to Y% of your pay.” With a 50% match up to 6%, your employer adds 50 cents for every dollar you contribute on the first 6% of your salary. The most they add is 3% of pay.

On an $80,000 salary with that formula:

You contributeYour dollarsEmployer matchTotal a year
3%$2,400$1,200$3,600
6%$4,800$2,400$7,200
10%$8,000$2,400$10,400

Contributing below the match cap leaves some match unclaimed. Contributing above it raises your savings, but the match stays at its maximum.

The calculator uses this formula: match = salary × min(your %, match cap %) × match rate. Plans set their own match formulas and conditions, so check your plan documents for the exact terms before relying on a figure.

The calculator adds the full match every year and assumes you keep all of it.

What are the 401(k) limits for 2026?

For 2026, you can defer up to $24,500 of your pay into a 401(k), up from $23,500 in 2025. If you are 50 or older, you can add a catch-up of $8,000. People aged 60 to 63 get a higher catch-up of $11,250 instead. The same limits apply to 403(b), governmental 457 plans and the federal Thrift Savings Plan.

Your age in 2026Deferral limitCatch-upTotal you can defer
Under 50$24,500—$24,500
50–59 or 64+$24,500$8,000$32,500
60–63$24,500$11,250$35,750

Source: IRS newsroom, IR-2025-111, November 13, 2025.

The IRS describes $24,500 as the limit on contributions by employees. The calculator applies it to your part only and adds the employer match on top. In the tests, a 45-year-old earning $300,000 who elects 15% ($45,000) is held to $24,500.

There is a new rule for higher earners in 2026. The IRS catch-up page (reviewed May 7, 2026) says that if your plan has a Roth option and your prior-year wages from the employer were over $150,000, your catch-up contributions must be made as Roth contributions. The calculator does not separate Roth and pre-tax money.

What is the catch-up for ages 60 to 63?

Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, employees who turn 60, 61, 62 or 63 during the year can make a larger catch-up contribution. The IRS says this began in 2025. For 2026 it is $11,250, instead of the standard $8,000, so the total you can defer at those ages is $35,750.

The higher amount stops applying in the year you turn 64. From then on, the standard $8,000 catch-up applies again. That makes ages 60 to 63 a four-year window with extra room to save.

The calculator applies the right limit for your age in each projected year. In its test case, a 61-year-old earning $500,000 who elects 50% is capped at $35,750: $24,500 plus $11,250.

For SIMPLE plans the figures differ. The IRS lists a $4,000 standard catch-up for 2026 and $5,250 for ages 60 to 63. This calculator uses the 401(k) figures only.

Frequently asked questions

How much will $10,000 in a 401(k) be worth in 20 years?

With no new contributions, $10,000 grows to about $32,071 in 20 years at an assumed 6% yearly return, or about $38,697 at 7%. At 5% it would be about $26,533. These are before fees and taxes, and real returns vary from year to year.

How much do I need in a 401(k) to get $2,000 a month?

$2,000 a month is $24,000 a year. Using a 4% first-year withdrawal rate as a planning assumption, that takes about $600,000. A lower rate needs more. Taxes on withdrawals, which depend on the type of money in the account, would reduce what you keep, so plan for them too.

Does your 401(k) double every 7 years?

Only at a return of about 10% a year. By the rule of 72, doubling in 7 years needs roughly 72 ÷ 7, or about 10.3%. At 7% a balance takes about 10.2 years to double, and at 6% about 11.9 years. Contributions speed up growth, but returns are never guaranteed.

How do I calculate how much my 401(k) will be worth?

Each year, grow the balance by your return, then add your contribution (capped at the IRS limit) and your employer match. Repeat until retirement. The calculator above does this for you, using the 2026 limits and the salary, contribution rate, match and return you enter.
