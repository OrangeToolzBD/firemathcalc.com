Roth IRA Calculator

This Roth IRA calculator projects your tax-free balance and checks your 2026 limit. The IRS cap is $7,500, plus $1,100 at 50 or older (IR-2025-111, November 13, 2025). Starting at 30 with $10,000 and adding $7,500 a year at an assumed 6% return, you would have about $912,622 by 65.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Fire Math Calc

How much will my Roth IRA be worth?

Your future balance depends on what you have now, what you add each year, the years left and the return you assume. The calculator grows the balance by your return each year and adds your contribution at year end. It first caps that contribution at your allowed 2026 limit. Qualified withdrawals come out tax-free, so the result is the amount you would keep.

Worked example, using the defaults:

  • Age 30, withdrawing at 65, so 35 years of growth.
  • $10,000 already in the account.
  • $7,500 a year, filing single, with $90,000 modified AGI (below the phase-out, so the full limit applies).
  • An assumed 6% yearly return.

Result: about $912,622 at 65. Of that, $272,500 is money you put in ($10,000 plus 35 × $7,500). The rest is growth.

The calculator keeps contributions at the 2026 dollar limit for every future year. The IRS adjusts limits over time, so this is a simplification. The return is your assumption and is not guaranteed.

The Roth tax benefit depends on rules the tool does not check, such as whether a withdrawal is a qualified distribution. The IRS Roth IRA page (reviewed September 8, 2026) confirms three points: contributions are not deductible, qualified distributions are tax-free, and the original owner has no required minimum distributions. As the IRS puts it, you can leave money in the account “as long as you live.”

What is the Roth IRA contribution limit for 2026?

For 2026, you can contribute up to $7,500 to a Roth IRA. Contributions to traditional IRAs for the same year reduce that amount, so the cap applies across all your IRAs together. If you are 50 or older, the catch-up adds $1,100, for a total of $8,600. These figures come from the IRS announcement of November 13, 2025. The limits were $7,000 and $1,000 in 2025.

2026 IRA limitAmount
Under 50$7,500
50 or older (with $1,100 catch-up)$8,600

Two other caps apply. First, your contribution cannot exceed your taxable compensation for the year, as IRS Publication 590-A explains. Second, your income may reduce or remove your Roth limit, as the next section shows.

The calculator checks your age for the catch-up and your income for the phase-out. It does not check compensation, so enter a contribution you are eligible to make.

The 2026 figures were announced in November 2025. This site checks for the following year’s limits each November and updates the calculator when they are published. See the methodology page for the refresh schedule.

What happens if my income is in the phase-out range?

Inside the phase-out range, your Roth IRA limit shrinks gradually as your modified AGI rises. Above the range, you cannot make direct Roth contributions for the year. For 2026, the IRS ranges are:

Filing status2026 phase-out range (modified AGI)
Single or head of household$153,000 – $168,000
Married filing jointly$242,000 – $252,000
Married filing separately$0 – $10,000

For 2025, the ranges were $150,000 to $165,000 for single filers and $236,000 to $246,000 for joint filers.

The calculator estimates a reduced limit on a straight line: limit × (top of range − your MAGI) ÷ width of range. For a single filer under 50 with $160,500 MAGI: $7,500 × ($168,000 − $160,500) ÷ $15,000 = $3,750. Below $153,000 the full $7,500 applies, and at $168,000 or above it is $0.

The official method is Worksheet 2-2 in Publication 590-A (the 2025 edition was current when this page was written). It follows the same idea but rounds the result up to the next $10 and applies a $200 minimum when the result is above zero. Confirm your exact figure there before contributing.

The tool warns you when you are inside or above the range. It then caps your projected contributions at the estimated amount.

Roth IRA vs traditional IRA

The main difference is when you pay tax. With a traditional IRA, contributions may be deductible now, and withdrawals of deductible contributions and earnings are taxed later. With a Roth IRA, contributions are not deductible, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Both share the same $7,500 limit for 2026.

Traditional IRARoth IRA
ContributionsDeductible if you qualifyNot deductible
Qualified withdrawalsTaxed (deductible contributions and earnings)Tax-free
Required minimum distributionsYesNot for the original owner
Effect of incomeThe deduction can phase out if you or your spouse has a workplace planThe contribution limit phases out (2026 ranges above)

Source: IRS, Traditional and Roth IRAs (reviewed January 8, 2026).

For 2026, the traditional IRA deduction phases out between $81,000 and $91,000 of modified AGI for single filers covered by a workplace plan. For joint filers, it is $129,000 to $149,000 when the contributing spouse is covered.

Which account suits you depends partly on whether you expect a higher or lower tax rate in retirement than today. Consider comparing both with a tax professional. Frequently asked questions

How much does a Roth IRA grow in 10 years?

It depends on your contributions and returns. Investing $7,500 at the end of each year for 10 years at an assumed 6% return grows to about $98,856, from $75,000 of contributions. At other returns the answer changes, so try a few rates in the calculator. Returns are never guaranteed.

How much should I put in my Roth IRA per month?

To reach the 2026 limit of $7,500, you would contribute $625 a month, or about $716.67 a month if you are 50 or older and use the $8,600 limit. Whether that fits your budget, and whether the Roth is the right account for you, depends on your situation. Your compensation and income must also allow it.

What happens if you put $7,000 per year in a Roth IRA?

At an assumed 6% yearly return, $7,000 invested at the end of each year grows to about $92,266 after 10 years and about $553,407 after 30 years. You would have contributed $70,000 and $210,000. Qualified withdrawals of that balance are tax-free under IRS rules.

Can I put $100,000 into a Roth IRA?

Not as a regular yearly contribution. For 2026 the limit is $7,500, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older, and it may be lower if your income is in the phase-out range. Publication 590-A says a 6% excise tax applies to excess Roth contributions. Moving larger sums into a Roth usually involves a rollover or conversion, which has its own tax rules.
